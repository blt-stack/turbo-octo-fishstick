# Pricing Tier Request Letter

Ask the supplier for a better unit price — by requesting an explicit volume-tier breakdown, by referencing a future volume commitment, or by negotiating against another quote (carefully).

## Why pricing is hard to negotiate

Supplier margins on small runs are thin; large runs are where they make money. Most suppliers genuinely cannot drop unit price without one of:
- **Volume commitment** — they only break the price at the higher tier
- **Order frequency** — 4 orders/year commitment vs. one-off
- **Spec simplification** — drop a feature, drop the cost
- **Material substitution** — different (cheaper) raw material
- **Payment up-front** — they can offer ~2-5% discount for 100% deposit (they save financing cost)
- **Longer lead time** — let them slot into off-peak production

So a successful pricing tier ask is almost never "please give me a discount" — it's "here's what I can offer; here's what unit price we'd hit; can we make that work?"

## The pricing tier 5-part letter

### 1. Open

Regional opener (see region playbook).

### 2. Bridge

Reference the most recent quote or invoice:

> "Thank you for the quote sent on [date] for [product] at [$X] per unit at [Y] units."

### 3. Ask

Three common framings:

**Volume-tier breakdown ask** (most professional):
> "Could you share your full pricing tier breakdown? We'd like to understand how the unit price moves at 500, 1000, 2500, and 5000 units. This will help us plan our growth orders."

This isn't a discount ask — it's information. But once you have the tiers, you can plan.

**Volume commitment ask:**
> "We're planning four orders of 1500 units across the year. If we commit to 6000 units annually, what unit price could we work toward? Happy to structure as a blanket order or scheduled releases."

**Payment-term-for-price-break ask:**
> "Would you offer a discount for 100% upfront payment? Our cash position allows it for the right pricing."

**Matching-a-quote ask** (use carefully):
> "We have a comparable quote at $X per unit for a similar product. We'd much rather work with you given our relationship — would you be open to matching, or to discussing what unit price would work given our planned volume?"

Never name the competitor by name. Never reveal the actual quote — just the per-unit number.

### 4. Mutual benefit framing

Tie the ask to predictable volume the supplier can plan against:

> "Stable volume planning helps both sides — you can slot us into your production schedule reliably, and we can build our promotional calendar around guaranteed lead times."

### 5. Close

Specific next step:

> "Could you send the tier breakdown by [date]? Or, if you'd prefer, happy to discuss on a call."

## Length targets

Same as MOQ letter:
- China / Vietnam: 180-280 words
- India: 140-220 words
- North America / EU: 120-180 words

## What NEVER goes in a pricing letter

- The specific number of a competitor's quote ("I have a $3.50 quote from another supplier") — invites the supplier to feel commoditized
- The competitor's name
- Implication that the supplier's price is "high" or "unreasonable"
- "We're a small brand so..." — every supplier hears this and discounts the ask
- Discount asks framed as personal ("Could you help us out?") — keep it business

## Negative signals to listen for

- **"Our price is fixed"** — supplier sees the ask as a commodity-shopper move. Soften and re-approach with a structured volume commit.
- **"We can do 5% if you pay 100% upfront"** — healthy counter; evaluate against cash position.
- **"We can offer a different model at $X"** — they're protecting margin by switching SKUs. Evaluate whether the alt SKU meets the brand standard.
- **Slow / no reply**: the ask landed wrong, especially in China. Consider a softer follow-up.

## Worked example — established China supplier, asking for volume tier breakdown

```
Subject: Pricing planning for 2026 — tier breakdown request

Dear Ms. Chen,

Thank you for the smooth shipment of order #5118 last week. We're now planning our 2026 ordering schedule and wanted to ask about your pricing tier structure.

For the River Stone Pour-Over, we have your quote at $4.20/unit at 1000 units. As we plan growth orders for 2026, could you share the full tier breakdown? We'd like to understand:

- 1500 units per order
- 2500 units per order
- 5000 units per order (annual commitment)

Knowing these tiers will help us build our promotional calendar around the price points and commit accordingly. We're projecting 4 orders in 2026, so if a blanket annual commitment makes more sense than per-order pricing, we're open to that structure too.

Could you send the tier breakdown by April 15? Or if a quick call is easier, I'm available next week any morning your time.

Best regards,
Jamie Carter
Stone & Glass
```

## Worked example — NA supplier, established relationship, payment-for-discount ask

```
Subject: Payment structure question for next order

Hi Mike,

Quick question on the upcoming order. We're at a cash position where we can pay 100% upfront if there's room to talk pricing. Currently we're at NET 30 with you which works well — but if a per-unit discount is available for 100% pre-pay, we'd take that for the next order or two.

What's your view? If it's not a fit on your end, no worries — happy to stay at NET 30 with current pricing.

Thanks,
Jamie
```

Short, direct, gives the supplier an easy out. Classic NA register.
