# Letter Craft — Universal Structure

Every supplier letter, regardless of type or region, follows the same 5-part skeleton. Letter type + region + relationship stage shape *what fills each part*, but the structure holds.

## The 5-part skeleton

1. **Open** — regional greeting + relationship acknowledgment
2. **Bridge** — connect to context (prior order, recent message, situation)
3. **Ask** — the specific request, framed per region + relationship stage
4. **Mutual benefit framing** — why this works for the supplier too
5. **Close** — clear next step + sign-off

## Why the structure matters

A supplier letter that lacks a bridge reads as transactional ("I want this"). A supplier letter that lacks a mutual-benefit framing reads as one-sided ("I want, you give"). A supplier letter that lacks a close reads as drift ("...so, anyway").

Each part has a job. Skip a part and the supplier's reply gets worse.

## Relationship stage adjustments

The skill tracks relationship stage in the supplier memory cache (`new` / `established` / `strained`). Each stage gets different treatment:

### New supplier

- Warmer intro (introduce who you are)
- More relationship-building in the bridge
- Smaller asks (don't open with payment-term changes)
- More mutual-benefit framing (supplier needs to know why they should invest in you)
- Specific timeline expectations
- Often pair with "looking forward to building a long-term relationship"

### Established supplier (3+ completed orders)

- Skip the intro; reference prior orders specifically
- Leaner bridge (one sentence is enough)
- Can ask bigger things (payment terms, larger pricing tiers, custom specs)
- Less mutual-benefit framing needed
- Can be more specific about timing (e.g., "for our Q3 launch")

### Strained supplier (recent dispute, missed deadline, quality issue)

- Acknowledge the friction at the start (don't pretend nothing happened)
- Bridge may include a relationship-mend sentence
- Often skip combined asks — too much pressure on a strained relationship
- More measured tone, fewer exclamations
- Avoid asks that compound the strain (e.g., don't ask for better payment terms right after a quality dispute)
- Consider whether the ask should wait until the prior issue resolves

## Combined-ask rules

When pairing two asks in one letter:

| Combination | Allowed? | Order |
|---|---|---|
| MOQ + sample | Yes — natural pairing | Sample first, then MOQ |
| MOQ + payment terms | Established relationships only | MOQ first |
| MOQ + pricing tier | Discouraged on first orders | If pairing, MOQ first |
| Pricing tier + payment terms | Yes, for established | Pricing first |
| Sample + payment terms | No — too early in relationship to ask both |
| Dispute + anything else | Never. Dispute is a standalone letter. |
| Pricing tier + custom spec | Established + needs care | Pricing first, custom spec as "and would this affect pricing" |

When in doubt, send one letter at a time. The supplier respects you more for clarity than for efficiency.

## Subject line conventions

Subject lines vary by letter type:

| Letter type | Subject line pattern |
|---|---|
| MOQ reduction | "Following up on the [product] quote" or "MOQ question for [product]" |
| Pricing tier | "Pricing planning for 2026 — tier breakdown request" or "Pricing structure question" |
| Payment terms | "Payment terms request for upcoming orders" |
| Sample request | "Sample request — [product] evaluation" |
| Dispute | "Order #[id] — quality issue on [product]" or "Order #[id] — shipment short / spec mismatch" |
| Follow-up after silence | "Re: [original subject]" or "Following up: [original subject]" |

Avoid:
- "URGENT" — overused; loses meaning
- "Quick question" — invites a slow reply ("just a small ask")
- All caps anywhere
- Multiple subjects in one email (one ask per email)

## Numeric specificity

Be specific. Suppliers value clarity. Numbers that should appear in letters:

- **Quantities** — "250 units," "1,500 units annually"
- **Prices** — "$4.20 per unit at 1,000 units"
- **Percentages** — "30% deposit + 70% balance," "14% defective"
- **Dates** — "by April 15," "received March 12"
- **Lead times** — "30-day production lead time," "shipped within 14 days of deposit"
- **Tolerances** — "±2mm on the rim diameter"

Vagueness reads as inexperience. "A few units" or "soon" or "small order" don't work.

## What NEVER goes in any supplier letter

- Emotional language ("I'm furious," "this is terrible")
- Threats of legal action in first contact (escalate only)
- Public reputation threats ("I'll tell the world about this")
- Personal hardship framing ("I really need this to work for my family")
- Vague timelines ("when you can," "in the next while")
- Asking the supplier to "trust" you on numbers without offering documentation
- Comparing to competitors by name
- Suggesting their team is incompetent ("clearly there's a quality problem with your team")
- Multiple unrelated asks bundled
- Promises you can't keep ("we'll order 10,000 units next year" when you don't know that)

## Alternate phrasings

Every letter benefits from having 2-3 alternate phrasings for the key ask sentence. The skill outputs these in the .docx so the buyer can pick what fits their voice. Example:

For an MOQ ask, alternates might be:
- "Would 300 units be workable as a starting point?"
- "We'd like to propose a first run of 300 units."
- "Could you let us know whether 300 units is viable?"

Same meaning, different voice register. The buyer's brand voice determines which lands right.

## The ear test

Before output, the skill reads each letter through:
- Does the open feel natural for the region?
- Is the bridge specific to context, or generic?
- Is the ask clear in one sentence?
- Does the mutual-benefit framing land?
- Does the close commit to a specific next step?

If any answer is no, redraft that section.

## Voice calibration to brand archetype

Even supplier letters carry brand voice. Adjust:

- **Craft / Maker archetype** — slightly warmer relationship language, references to quality
- **Performance / Athlete archetype** — more direct, more numbers, less relationship preamble
- **Wellness / Care archetype** — softer overall, more relationship signals
- **Minimalist / Modern archetype** — leaner sentences, clear structure
- **Playful / Indie archetype** — keep professional in supplier correspondence (the playful voice is for customers, not suppliers); slight warmth ok

## When the buyer wants to override the structure

The skill defaults to the 5-part structure but accepts override requests:

- "Make it shorter" — collapse open + bridge into one sentence; trim mutual-benefit to one phrase
- "Make it more formal" — apply +1 formality from the region's default
- "Less relationship preamble" — skip the warmth signals (useful when buyer is annoyed with the supplier)
- "More urgent" — adjust the close to faster deadline, add "given timing" framing

The skill flags when an override pushes the letter outside best-practice for the region.
