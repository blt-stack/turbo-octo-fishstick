# Region: China

The most common supplier region for solo e-commerce operators. Conventions differ meaningfully from Western business communication; getting them right is the difference between fast replies and silence.

## Opening conventions

### Honorifics

- **"Dear Ms. [Lastname]"** or **"Dear Mr. [Lastname]"** — most common; use unless told otherwise
- **"Dear [Firstname]"** acceptable after the contact has signed an email with just their first name
- **"Hi [Firstname]"** only after 5+ exchanges and signal from supplier

Common surnames: Chen, Liu, Wang, Zhang, Li, Zhou, Wu, Xu, Sun, Ma, Zhu. Many factory reps use English first names ("Cindy," "Jack," "Lily") + Chinese surnames — use both.

### Opening line

Acceptable openers, in order of warmth:

1. **"Dear Ms. Chen, hope you are well."** — neutral, professional
2. **"Dear Ms. Chen, I hope this email finds you well."** — slightly more formal
3. **"Dear Ms. Chen, hope you had a good [holiday name] holiday."** — when timing applies; very warm
4. **"Dear Ms. Chen, hope your factory is running smoothly this week."** — relational, only for established relationships

### Avoid in openings

- "Hi!" — too casual
- "Greetings" — sounds like a chatbot
- "I hope you are doing well during these challenging times" — pandemic-era cliché
- Starting with the ask — extremely abrupt by Chinese business norms

## Pacing of asks

**Lead with relationship and context. Bury the ask in the middle.** Chinese business correspondence values harmony — getting to the ask too quickly reads as transactional in a way that hurts the relationship.

The 5-part letter is structured for this:

1. Open (warm)
2. Bridge (context / past order / relationship)
3. Ask (the specific request)
4. Mutual benefit (why this works for both sides)
5. Close (specific next step)

Western templates often invert this — ask first, justify later. Don't.

## Numeric specificity

Be specific but soften the framing:

- Western: "We need 250 units."
- China: "We were hoping to start with 250 units. Would this be possible?"

The information conveyed is the same; the framing acknowledges that the supplier has discretion.

## Honorifics in body

- Refer to supplier's company respectfully — "your factory," "your team," "your QC department"
- Avoid "you guys"
- Avoid imperatives ("Send me a quote") — use "Could you," "Would you be open to," "We'd like to ask"

## Pacing expectations

Reply windows in China:
- Same business day to next business day: high-engagement supplier, you're a priority customer
- 24-72 hours: normal pace
- 3-5 business days: supplier is slow; the relationship may be cooling
- 5+ business days without reply: assume something is wrong; soft follow-up needed

Beijing time business hours: 9 AM - 6 PM, Monday-Friday. Many factories observe Saturday morning work.

## Holidays — critical

The skill checks dates against these and warns the buyer:

| Holiday | Approximate dates | Impact |
|---|---|---|
| Lunar New Year (Spring Festival) | Late January or early February (varies; 7-15 days closed) | Factories close 1-2 weeks; supply chain delays for 4-6 weeks before and after |
| Qingming Festival | Early April (1-3 days) | Minor |
| Labor Day | May 1 + surrounding days (3-5 days) | Moderate |
| Dragon Boat Festival | Late May or June (3 days) | Minor |
| Mid-Autumn Festival | September or October (3 days) | Minor |
| National Day / Golden Week | October 1-7 (7 days) | Major — factories often closed full week + recovery time |

Sending a critical letter the week before Spring Festival or Golden Week is poor timing. The skill recommends rescheduling to after the holiday return.

## Indirect framing — when and how

For sensitive asks (pricing pressure, payment term changes, dispute escalation), Chinese business culture rewards indirect framing:

- Direct: "Your price is too high."
- Indirect: "We've been analyzing our cost structure for the launch — at the current unit price, we're finding the margin tight. Could we discuss whether there's flexibility at our planned volume?"

The indirect version conveys the same constraint without putting the supplier in a face-losing position.

## Mianzi (面子) — "face"

The concept of face matters in Chinese business. Avoid:

- Publicly suggesting the supplier failed (use private framing)
- Direct contradiction in early conversation
- Insisting on Western contract enforcement language in early emails
- Threats of finding another supplier in a way that humiliates them

Acknowledge their effort, frame disputes as misunderstandings to be resolved, give them an out even when you're firm.

## Sign-off

- **"Best regards, [First Last]"** — standard, professional
- **"Kind regards, [First Last]"** — slightly warmer, also common
- **"Thank you for your continued partnership, [First Last]"** — for established relationships
- **"Sincerely, [First Last]"** — slightly formal, acceptable

Avoid: "Cheers," "Talk soon," "Catch you later" — too casual.

## Length targets in China region

- New supplier first contact: 220-300 words
- Established supplier routine ask: 180-260 words
- Dispute letter: 280-400 words (more documentation, more relationship-mending)
- Quick follow-up after silence: 80-140 words

## TradeMessenger vs. direct email

Alibaba TradeMessenger is the entry point for most Chinese supplier relationships. After 2-3 exchanges, most reps will share a direct email — switch to email at that point. Email is more professional and less rate-limited than TradeMessenger.

When drafting for TradeMessenger:
- Keep paragraphs shorter (chat-style)
- Acceptable to send a longer letter as a "I'm sending a longer message — please read" preamble
- Major terms changes (payment, dispute) should always move to email

## Phrases that work specifically well in China

- "We're a growing brand looking for a long-term manufacturing partner."
- "We believe this is the foundation of a strong working relationship."
- "We appreciate the care you've put into this order."
- "Looking forward to building this partnership."

These all signal long-term intent, which is what Chinese suppliers reward.

## Phrases that DON'T translate

- "Touching base" — confusing translation
- "Circling back" — same
- "Ping me when..." — too casual
- "Let me know your bandwidth" — confusing
- "Out of pocket" — extremely confusing (translates literally)
- Idioms in general — keep language plain
