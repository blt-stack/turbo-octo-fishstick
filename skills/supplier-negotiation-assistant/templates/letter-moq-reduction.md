# MOQ Reduction Letter

The most common ask for solo operators. Supplier says "MOQ is 1000." You want 250. The letter's job is to get a reduced MOQ without making the supplier feel like you're cheap or unserious.

## Why MOQs exist (so you can negotiate them effectively)

Suppliers set MOQs based on:
- **Setup cost recovery** — printing plates, mold/tool depreciation, line changeover
- **Material minimum purchase** — they buy raw materials in lots; smaller orders don't divide cleanly
- **Margin viability** — small orders are less profitable per labor-hour
- **Risk hedging** — they don't want to set up a line for a one-time tiny customer

Knowing this, the leverage points are:
1. **Pay for the setup cost yourself** (a setup-fee + lower MOQ)
2. **Commit to recurring orders** (lower MOQ if you commit to N orders/year)
3. **Order an existing SKU or stock material** (no setup cost = lower MOQ negotiable)
4. **Pay a per-unit premium** (higher unit price in exchange for lower MOQ)
5. **Combine with existing production runs** (your 250 units rides along on a 5,000-unit run)

The letter mentions ONE of these leverage points, not all five. Leading with all five reads as desperate.

## The MOQ reduction 5-part letter

### 1. Open (region-shaped)

China: relationship-warm; ask about their last order context
NA/EU: efficient greeting; can skip relationship preamble after the first exchange
India: warm but more direct than China
Vietnam: warm + slightly formal

### 2. Bridge

Reference the prior conversation or quoted MOQ. Something like:
> "Thank you for the quote you sent on [date] for [product]. The 1000-unit MOQ is higher than our launch plan supports."

This frames the issue as your constraint, not their failing.

### 3. Ask (with leverage point)

Pick ONE leverage point:

**Setup-fee approach:**
> "Would it be possible to do an initial run of 250 units with a setup fee on our side? We'd like to commit to two follow-on orders within six months, at 500 units each."

**Commit-to-recurring approach:**
> "We'd like to start with 250 units, and can commit to four orders per year. If we hit the 1000-unit annual volume, would the MOQ work as a quarterly threshold rather than a per-order threshold?"

**Per-unit-premium approach:**
> "We understand smaller runs may need a different unit price. Would you be open to a 250-unit run at a premium (perhaps the 100-unit price tier from your sheet)?"

**Existing-SKU approach:**
> "Looking at your catalog, [SKU] is very close to what we need. If we ordered [SKU] with [minor customization], would a lower MOQ work?"

**Combine-with-production approach:**
> "We're flexible on timing. If 250 units could ride along with one of your scheduled production runs, we're happy to wait 6-8 weeks."

### 4. Mutual benefit framing

Critical in Asian regions; useful everywhere.

> "We're a growing brand and looking for a long-term manufacturing partner. Starting smaller lets us validate the product with our customers before scaling — which is when both of us benefit most."

The implicit message: this isn't the last order; help us start small and we'll grow together.

### 5. Close

Specific next step + soft deadline:

> "Could you let us know by [date — 5-7 business days out in China, 3-4 in NA] whether 250 units is workable? Happy to discuss the structure that works best on your end."

Sign with first name + brand. Never sign as "Procurement Team" for a solo operation — suppliers in Asia especially read that as larger than the brand actually is, and the mismatch hurts trust later.

## Length targets

- China / Vietnam: 180-280 words (longer, more relationship)
- India: 140-220 words
- North America / EU: 120-180 words

## Leverage points to NEVER use in writing

- "Your competitor is offering it cheaper" — works face-to-face, reads as adversarial in email
- Threats of finding another supplier — burns the relationship
- Vague promises of "huge volume in the future" — every supplier hears this and discounts it
- Personal hardship framing — unprofessional, especially in Asia

## Combined-ask considerations

When pairing MOQ with another ask (e.g., payment terms or pricing), MOQ is usually the lead ask because it's the most concrete and easiest to grant.

| Pairing | Approach |
|---|---|
| MOQ + payment terms | Lead with MOQ; payment terms as "and given the smaller initial commitment, we'd like to keep the deposit at 30%" |
| MOQ + pricing tier | Don't pair on first order — ask for MOQ now, pricing tier after first order delivered |
| MOQ + sample | Often natural pairing — sample request precedes the MOQ ask in time |
| MOQ + custom specs | Pair carefully; custom specs work against MOQ reduction since they add setup cost |

## Negative signals to listen for in the reply

- **No counter-offer, just restating the original MOQ**: supplier sees the relationship as too small to bend on
- **Counter at 800 instead of 250**: they want the business but need cost recovery; negotiate further
- **Counter at 500 with a unit price increase**: they're matching your ask with a per-unit premium — this is a healthy outcome
- **"We can do 250 if you pay setup fee of $X"**: ideal counter — they're solving the problem you proposed solving
- **Long silence (5+ days for non-holiday China, 2+ days for NA)**: the ask may have annoyed them; consider a softer follow-up
- **"Let me discuss with our sales manager"**: positive signal in China — the front-line rep is willing to escalate, which means they want the business

## Worked example — China supplier, new relationship

**Context:** Buyer found a ceramic supplier in Jingdezhen via Alibaba. Supplier quoted $4.20/unit at 1000 MOQ. Buyer wants 300 for an initial launch.

```
Subject: Following up on the River Stone Pour-Over quote

Dear Ms. Chen,

Thank you for the detailed quote you sent on March 14 for the River Stone Pour-Over. The 1000-unit MOQ is higher than our launch plan can support, so I'd like to ask about a smaller initial run.

Would it be possible to do a first order of 300 units? We understand smaller production may not match the 1000-unit unit price — we'd be comfortable at the 100-unit price tier from your sheet ($4.80/unit) for this initial run. We're committed to scaling: our plan is to reorder within 3 months at 500+ units, and if the product performs the way we expect, we'd be at the 1000-unit threshold by the end of 2026.

We're looking for a long-term partner in ceramics, and starting at 300 lets us validate the design with our customers before committing more. We believe this is the foundation of a strong working relationship.

Could you let us know by March 28 if 300 units at the 100-tier price is workable? Happy to discuss any alternatives — including a setup fee or rolling our order into one of your scheduled production windows.

Best regards,
Jamie Carter
Stone & Glass
```

## Worked example — North America supplier, established relationship

**Context:** US supplier, 4 prior orders. Buyer wants to add a new SKU but the supplier's standard MOQ for new tooling is 2000 units. Buyer wants 500.

```
Subject: New SKU — request to reduce MOQ for initial run

Hi Mike,

Hope all's well. We're ready to launch the next product (the satchel I sent specs on last week) and want to do an initial run of 500 units rather than your standard 2000 for new tooling.

We'd cover the tooling cost upfront separately ($X — confirm with your quote) and run at whatever per-unit price works for the 500-unit volume. Once the launch sells through (we're projecting 90 days), we'll reorder at 1500+ which puts us at your normal pricing.

Can you let me know by Friday if this structure works? Happy to jump on a call if it's easier.

Thanks,
Jamie
```

Notice: NA letter is ~50% shorter, has a specific deadline, includes a phone-call offer, and doesn't lead with relationship language because the relationship is already established.
