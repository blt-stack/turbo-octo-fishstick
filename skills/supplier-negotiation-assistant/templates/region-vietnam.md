# Region: Vietnam

Vietnamese suppliers are a common alternative to Chinese ones — particularly for textiles, apparel, furniture, and home goods. Communication conventions share some elements with both China (formality, relationship-aware) and South-East Asia generally (slightly more flexible than China, more relationship than India).

## Opening conventions

### Honorifics

- **"Dear Mr. [Lastname]"** or **"Dear Ms. [Lastname]"** — standard for first contact
- Vietnamese names: family name comes first ("Nguyen Van Anh" — Nguyen is the family name, but Vietnamese custom in Western communication often inverts to "Anh Nguyen"). When unsure, ask politely how the supplier prefers to be addressed.
- Common family names: Nguyen, Tran, Le, Pham, Hoang, Phan, Vu, Vo, Dang, Bui

### English fluency

English fluency varies significantly in Vietnam — wider range than in India. Some suppliers communicate at near-native fluency; others use translation tools and short phrasing. Match the supplier's register:

- If the supplier writes in long, fluent paragraphs → respond similarly
- If the supplier writes short, simple sentences → respond similarly; avoid idioms and complex syntax

### Opening line

1. **"Dear Mr. Nguyen, hope you are well."** — standard
2. **"Dear Mr. Nguyen, hope all is well at your factory."** — relational
3. **"Dear Mr. Nguyen, hope you and your family are well."** — warm, common in Vietnamese business culture

## Pacing of asks

Vietnamese business culture sits between Chinese (very indirect) and Indian (fairly direct). Lead with context, but the bridge can be shorter than in China.

The 5-part letter applies; bridge is typically 2 sentences in Vietnam vs. 3-4 in China.

## Numeric specificity

Be specific but maintain some softening:

- "Our planned order is 300 units. Is this workable on your side?"
- "Could you let us know if 1500 units at $X is the right price point?"

Less softening than China, more than Vietnam-direct.

## Pacing expectations

Reply windows in Vietnam:
- 24-48 hours: normal pace
- 48-72 hours: somewhat slow but acceptable
- 72+ hours: nudge gently

Vietnam business hours: 8 AM - 5 PM ICT (UTC+7), Monday-Saturday (half day Saturday common).

## Holidays — significant

| Holiday | Approximate dates | Impact |
|---|---|---|
| Lunar New Year (Tet) | Late January or early February (7-10 days) | Major — entire country shuts down for ~1 week; supply chain delays for 3-4 weeks |
| Reunification Day | April 30 | 1 day; often paired with May 1 for a long weekend |
| Labor Day | May 1 | 1 day |
| National Day | September 2 | 1-2 days |
| Mid-Autumn Festival | September or October | Light impact |

Tet is the major shutdown. Sending a major letter the week before Tet is poor timing.

## Honorifics in body

- "Sir" and "Madam" used moderately — less than India
- Avoid the supplier's first name + family name reversal unless they signed that way themselves
- Refer to their company as "your factory" or "your team"

## Mutual benefit framing

Vietnamese suppliers respond well to long-term relationship framing similar to China:

- "We see this as a long-term partnership."
- "Building stable orders together benefits both sides."
- "We value the consistency we've found with your factory."

## Sign-off

- **"Best regards"** — most common
- **"Kind regards"** — also common
- **"Warm regards"** — slightly warmer
- **"Sincerely"** — formal, fine

## Length targets in Vietnam region

- New supplier first contact: 200-280 words
- Established supplier routine ask: 160-240 words
- Dispute letter: 260-380 words
- Quick follow-up after silence: 70-130 words

## Common nuance — relationship beats contract

Vietnamese business culture, like Chinese, rewards relationship over contract enforcement. Don't lead with legal language even in disputes. Lead with "we want to understand what happened" — and only escalate to documentation/contract language if the supplier doesn't respond.

## Phrases that work specifically well in Vietnam

- "We hope this is the start of a long working relationship."
- "We appreciate your team's effort on this order."
- "Looking forward to growing the volume together."
- "Thank you for your understanding."

## Phrases to avoid

Same Western idioms that don't translate to China: "touch base," "circle back," "bandwidth," etc.

Also: avoid implying speed-vs-quality tradeoffs in a way that pressures the supplier into faster-but-worse work — Vietnamese factories particularly will agree to faster timelines and then deliver lower quality, which can be the opposite of what you wanted. Be explicit that quality is the priority.
