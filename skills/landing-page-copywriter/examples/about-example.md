THE STORY (3 narrative beats)

Beat 1 — The frustration (160 words)
Sub-heading: "The pour-overs we could find were embarrassing."
In 2022, two of us were brewing coffee in a half-decent studio apartment with what was on
the market: glass drippers that cracked after six weeks, plastic ones that tasted like
the plastic, and ceramic ones that arrived warped from overseas. None of them held heat.
Most of them required paper filters, which meant a $20-a-month subscription to paper. We
kept calling each other to complain about it.

Beat 2 — The early experiments (160 words)
Sub-heading: "The first 40 looked like first 40s usually do."
We bought a small wheel off Craigslist and started throwing drippers in a kitchen corner.
The first dozen cracked in the kiln. The next dozen didn't hold their shape under hot water.
Around piece 30 we figured out the wall thickness that kept heat without making the dripper
feel like a paperweight. We weren't trying to make a brand — we just wanted one that worked
for ourselves.

Beat 3 — Where we are now (140 words)
Sub-heading: "The studio is still three people."
We added one full-time potter and one part-time studio manager. We don't outsource. We
don't dropship. Every piece is thrown, glazed, fired, and shipped from the same 800-square-
foot studio in southeast Portland. We make about 200 pieces a month. When demand outpaces
that, we ask people to wait. We'd rather make 200 we're proud of than 2,000 we aren't.

WHAT WE BELIEVE — 3 PRINCIPLES

Principle 1 — Materials over marketing (80 words)
We spend more on what's inside the box than we do on what convinces you to open it. The
stoneware we use costs 40% more than the industrial-grade ceramic in most pour-overs — and
lasts about twice as long. Most of our packaging is recycled cardboard with a hand-stamped
label, because the photo on the box doesn't change how the coffee tastes.

Principle 2 — Repair before replace (75 words)
If something we made breaks from normal use, we repair it or replace it free. We've sent
about 120 replacements in three years. We'd rather take the cost than have one piece end
up in landfill. The cork bases we attach are pressed, not glued, so they can be replaced
separately. Most of our customers have had their dripper for years.

Principle 3 — Small batches, slow scale (90 words)
We could probably double output by adding wheels and hiring. We've chosen not to, twice
now when we had the chance. Slow scale lets us know every piece by sight, fix issues
before they multiply, and keep the studio a place we actually like working in. It also
means we sometimes sell out and ask people to wait. We think that's the right trade.

## What's distinctive about an About page vs other page types

- **No conversion framework.** Narrative structure (3-5 beats), not PAS/AIDA/PASTOR.
- **No CTA-heavy blocks.** The page builds trust; the "what we make" block at the end re-routes warm visitors into the store, but isn't a hard sell.
- **Real photos are mandatory.** Stock photos kill the About page faster than any other page type.
- **Founder voice is welcomed.** First-person ("we", "our", "I started") works here, not on PDP.
- **The Story is the biggest block** — 400-1000 words. The rest of the page supports it.
- **Principles need evidence.** Generic claims ("we care about quality") get replaced with specific decisions ("we spend 40% more on stoneware than competitors do").
- **If the buyer has no story yet, ask 7 prompts.** Don't fabricate origin stories.

---

## What this exemplar deliberately gets right

- Hero headline opens with a specific moment, not "About Us"
- Story beats have stakes (the frustration), specifics (piece 30, 800 sq ft, 200/month), and arc
- Principles each have a concrete fact backing them up
- Behind-the-scenes mentions audible details (testing for cracks by sound) — earns trust through specificity
- Team grid is small and personal
- "What we make" funnels back into the store without being pushy
- No mission statement language. Anywhere.
