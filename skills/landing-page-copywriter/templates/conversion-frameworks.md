# Conversion Frameworks Library

Six frameworks, each with a one-line summary, when to use, a sequence of beats, an example application, and pitfalls. The skill picks the best fit based on page type and product fit, or uses whichever framework the buyer named.

These frameworks shape *how copy flows* through page blocks. The block structure itself comes from the page-architecture file. Frameworks are the storytelling layer.

---

## Framework 1: PAS — Problem, Agitate, Solve

**One-line:** Name the problem, deepen the pain, present the solution.

**When to use:** the product solves a clearly painful problem the shopper already knows they have. Default for problem-aware audiences.

**Best for:** supplements, pain-relief products, organization tools, productivity software, dating apps, anti-aging, sleep aids, anything where the shopper is already searching for a fix.

**Beat sequence:**
1. **Problem** — name the problem in the shopper's words
2. **Agitate** — describe what life with the problem actually feels like (specific, sensory)
3. **Solve** — introduce the product as the fix

**Example application (hero → value props → product detail):**

> **Problem (hero):** Your back hurts every morning before you've even gotten out of bed.
>
> **Agitate (sub-headline):** It's worse on the days you slept "well." You stretch in the bathroom. You think about your chiropractor. You schedule the day around the pain.
>
> **Solve (value props):** The Lumbar Support Pillow molds to your spine while you sleep. Most people stop waking up sore inside two weeks.

**Pitfalls:**
- Over-agitating reads as exploitative — keep it to 1-2 sentences of pain
- Skipping the agitate step makes the framework feel hollow
- Using PAS when the shopper isn't actually in pain (luxury goods, hobbyist purchases) feels mismatched

## Framework 2: AIDA — Attention, Interest, Desire, Action

**One-line:** Grab attention, build interest, create desire, prompt action.

**When to use:** the product is in a category where shoppers compare options. Default for spec-driven purchases.

**Best for:** mid-ticket goods (kitchen tools, electronics, fitness gear), comparison-heavy categories (mattresses, knives, software), feature-rich products.

**Beat sequence:**
1. **Attention** — hook that names the shopper's interest
2. **Interest** — build curiosity with one surprising or specific detail
3. **Desire** — paint the use case / outcome
4. **Action** — clear, specific CTA

**Example application:**

> **Attention (hero):** A pour-over that brews one perfect cup in under three minutes.
>
> **Interest (sub):** Hand-thrown ceramic that holds heat better than glass. Reusable mesh filter included. Dishwasher safe.
>
> **Desire (value props + story):** Imagine slow Saturday mornings: one cup, no carafe, no paper waste, no machine humming. Brew-pour-clean in five minutes total.
>
> **Action (CTA):** Order yours — ships within 2 business days.

**Pitfalls:**
- AIDA can feel formulaic in lifestyle categories where shoppers don't compare specs (treat as a guide, not a script)
- The "Action" step gets neglected — don't end with a soft "Learn more"; end with a verb-led purchase action

## Framework 3: BAB — Before, After, Bridge

**One-line:** Show the before state, show the after state, name what bridges them.

**When to use:** the product enables a transformation. Default for aspirational/lifestyle products.

**Best for:** skincare, fitness equipment, courses, home decor, anything where the shopper is buying a future version of themselves.

**Beat sequence:**
1. **Before** — the current frustrating state (specific, relatable)
2. **After** — the desired state (specific, sensory)
3. **Bridge** — the product/process that gets the shopper from Before to After

**Example application:**

> **Before (hero):** Your skin feels tight, flaky, and somehow still oily by 3pm.
>
> **After (sub):** Imagine touching your face in the afternoon and finding it soft, calm, and even.
>
> **Bridge (value props + product):** This is the moisturizer that does both — hyaluronic acid for hydration, niacinamide for the oil control, no fragrance or alcohol to irritate. Twice a day, two weeks to notice.

**Pitfalls:**
- The "After" step needs to be specific and sensory — vague aspirations ("you'll feel amazing") fail
- BAB requires a clear narrative arc — if the product's transformation is subtle, FAB or JTBD work better

## Framework 4: JTBD — Jobs To Be Done

**One-line:** Name the job the shopper is hiring the product to do, the constraints, the expected outcome.

**When to use:** the product is hired (by the shopper) for a specific job. Default for tools, kitchenware, productivity, anything functional.

**Best for:** office gear, kitchen tools, parenting gear, hobbyist equipment, B2B tools, anything where the shopper has a clear use case in mind.

**Beat sequence:**
1. **Job** — what the shopper is trying to accomplish
2. **Constraints** — what the shopper needs the solution to respect (budget, space, time, ease)
3. **Outcome** — what success looks like

**Example application:**

> **Job (hero):** Brew one excellent cup of coffee in the morning, without the gear or the cleanup.
>
> **Constraints (sub):** No countertop machine. No paper filter purchases. Dishwasher-safe. Under $80.
>
> **Outcome (value props + story):** One cup in three minutes, brewed by hand, in a piece that lives on the counter and looks good doing it.

**Pitfalls:**
- JTBD works best when the buyer can articulate the "job" precisely. If the buyer doesn't know what job their product is hired for, use FAB or BAB instead.
- Over-engineering the JTBD framing makes the copy feel like a product-management deck. Keep it human.

## Framework 5: FAB — Features, Advantages, Benefits

**One-line:** For each feature, explain the advantage it gives, then the benefit the shopper feels.

**When to use:** the product is feature-rich and benefits aren't obvious from the features. Default for technical/spec-heavy products.

**Best for:** electronics, tools, supplements with ingredient stories, anything where features are the differentiator.

**Beat sequence (per feature):**
1. **Feature** — what the product has
2. **Advantage** — what that lets it do
3. **Benefit** — what the shopper experiences

**Example application (one value-prop bullet, FAB-shaped):**

> **Feature:** Stoneware body (vs. glass or plastic).
> **Advantage:** Holds brewing temperature 8°F warmer through a full pour.
> **Benefit:** Cleaner extraction — your coffee tastes brighter, with less bitterness.

In practice you don't usually surface all three layers visibly — you write the bullet at the benefit level and let the feature peek through. But the framework forces you to *justify* every claim with a real advantage and a real benefit.

**Pitfalls:**
- Page-wide FAB feels like a spec sheet — works best for the value-props block, not the whole page
- Skipping the "Advantage" middle layer is common; without it, the leap from feature to benefit feels unearned

## Framework 6: PASTOR — Person, Amplify, Story, Transformation, Offer, Response

**One-line:** Long-form persuasion sequence designed for cold-traffic landing pages.

**When to use:** marketing LPs with cold paid traffic. Default for ad-driven LPs.

**Best for:** info products, high-ticket physical products, course launches, anything where the shopper needs to be sold from cold.

**Beat sequence:**
1. **Person** — name the specific person you're talking to (in the headline or first paragraph)
2. **Amplify** — name the pain or aspiration in detail
3. **Story** — case study, testimonial, or origin story that earns credibility
4. **Transformation** — what the customer's life looks like after
5. **Offer** — the specific product/package/price with a risk-reversal
6. **Response** — clear, specific action the shopper takes now

**Example application (across LP sections):**

> **Person (hero):** If you're an apartment dweller who wants real coffee without a $400 machine on the counter...
>
> **Amplify (problem block):** Drip machines take up half the kitchen, French presses are messy, pour-overs require five pieces of gear, instant coffee tastes like sadness. You've tried it all.
>
> **Story (about block):** We were in the same apartment last year. Spent three months testing 14 pour-overs. Built this one because none of them worked the way we wanted.
>
> **Transformation (lifestyle block):** Wake up. Boil water. Brew one cup in three minutes. Rinse the dripper. Done. Your counter looks clean. Your coffee tastes alive.
>
> **Offer (purchase block):** River Stone Pour-Over + reusable filter + brewing guide. $68 today. 30-day money-back, ships within 2 business days.
>
> **Response (CTA):** Order yours now — we ship Fridays.

**Pitfalls:**
- PASTOR is long. Don't use it on a PDP — too much real estate spent on persuasion that organic shoppers don't need.
- The "Story" step requires real credibility — if you don't have a case study, an origin story, or a notable testimonial, PASTOR collapses.

## Framework selection logic (programmatic)

The skill picks a framework using this priority:

1. **User-specified** — if the user said "use PAS" or "give me a BAB version", honor it.
2. **Page type default:**
   - About → no framework (narrative)
   - Marketing LP → PASTOR (or PAS if PASTOR is too heavy)
   - Collection → FAB or AIDA
   - PDP → product-fit logic (below)
3. **Product-fit logic for PDP:**
   - Solves a known pain → PAS
   - Enables a transformation → BAB
   - Hired for a specific job → JTBD
   - Feature-rich + benefits non-obvious → FAB
   - Spec-driven comparison category → AIDA

The chosen framework is noted in the brief so the buyer knows which one was used and can request a different framework in a follow-up run.

---

## When to combine frameworks

Sometimes the page benefits from layering frameworks across blocks:

- Hero in PAS (name the pain)
- Value props in FAB (justify the features)
- Story block in narrative (no framework)
- Closing block in BAB (paint the transformation)

The chosen "primary" framework should dominate, but blocks can lean on different frameworks where it strengthens the argument. Don't switch frameworks mid-block — it confuses the read.
