# About / Brand Story Page Architecture

About pages have a different job from the other page types. They don't convert directly. They build trust, transfer brand values to the shopper, and turn a one-time buyer into someone who tells their friends about the brand. Treat them as the asset that makes every *other* page work harder.

About pages should *not* use named conversion frameworks. They use narrative structure — the same craft that powers good essays, founder letters, and Kickstarter campaigns.

---

## Block sequence (top to bottom)

1. **Hero — narrative opener** (not promotional)
2. **The story** (3-5 narrative beats)
3. **What we believe / Our principles** (3-5 stated values, with evidence)
4. **Behind the scenes** (process, materials, who-we-work-with)
5. **The team / The maker** (photo + bio for the founder, optional team grid)
6. **Press & recognition** (only if real, never invented)
7. **What we make** (curated entry points into the store)
8. **Sign-off + call to connect** (newsletter, social, get-in-touch)

---

## Block 1: Hero — narrative opener

The opposite of a marketing-LP hero. No CTA. No urgency. No product shot.

**Elements:**
- Headline — a phrase that opens the story (8-15 words). Not "About Us" — be specific.
- Sub-headline — one line that names what's coming (12-25 words).
- Hero image — typically the maker at work, the workspace, or the moment the brand began. Lifestyle, never catalog.

**Good headline examples:**
- "We started in a kitchen in Portland because no one was making honest pour-over gear."
- "Hand-thrown ceramics from a studio of three."
- "Built by a parent who couldn't find a stroller that fit a city sidewalk."

**Bad headline examples:**
- "About Us"
- "Our Story"
- "Welcome to [Brand]"

**Pitfall:** treating the About hero like a product hero. Different job. Different tone.

---

## Block 2: The story (3-5 narrative beats)

The core of the About page. 400-1000 words organized into 3-5 narrative beats. Each beat has a sub-heading + 1-3 paragraphs.

**Useful beat structures:**

**Founder origin (most common):**
1. The frustration — what the founder couldn't find or solve
2. The early experiments — what they tried that didn't work
3. The breakthrough — what changed
4. The decision to make it real — when they started selling
5. Where we are now — current scale / what's next

**Heritage / craft tradition:**
1. The tradition the brand draws from
2. The teacher / mentor / generation
3. What modern versions get wrong
4. How the brand does it differently
5. What customers do with it

**Problem-led / mission:**
1. The problem we exist to solve
2. The current state of the industry
3. Why no one was doing it right
4. Our approach
5. What success looks like

**Length per beat:** 80-200 words. Beats should flow — don't bullet-point them. Use paragraph prose.

**Conversion job:** transfer the founder's POV to the reader. The reader should finish feeling they know the brand and could explain it to a friend.

**Pitfalls:**
- Saccharine or self-congratulatory ("we're proud to be making a difference") — show, don't tell
- Mission-statement language ("our mission is to redefine the [category] industry") — translates to "we have no story"
- Resume mode ("Sarah, our founder, has 15 years of experience in...") — write a narrative, not a LinkedIn page
- Skipping the frustration — every good origin story names the problem before the solution. Without the frustration, there's no stakes.

---

## Block 3: What we believe / Our principles

3-5 stated values, each with a short paragraph of evidence. The values are how the brand makes decisions; the evidence is what readers can verify.

**Format per principle:**
- Principle name (3-7 words)
- 1-paragraph elaboration (60-100 words) — what this principle means in practice + a specific example

**Example:**

> **Materials over marketing**
> We spend more on what's inside the box than we do on what convinces you to open it. The stoneware we use costs 40% more than the industrial-grade ceramic in most pour-overs — and lasts about twice as long. Most of our packaging is recycled cardboard with a hand-stamped label, because the photo on the box doesn't change how the coffee tastes.

**Conversion job:** signal the brand's actual decision criteria, so the reader knows what they're buying into.

**Pitfalls:**
- Generic values that any brand could claim ("integrity", "quality", "innovation") — replace with specific principles
- Principles without evidence — claims with no proof are noise
- More than 5 principles — values blur together past 5

---

## Block 4: Behind the scenes

A visual + textual look at how things actually get made. 200-400 words.

**Possible content:**
- Workshop / studio / factory photos (real, never stock)
- Materials sourcing (where the wood comes from, where the cotton is woven, who dyes the leather)
- Process detail — one step explained well beats five steps mentioned briefly
- The makers (factory workers, artisans, suppliers — by name when possible and with permission)

**Conversion job:** make the abstract claim of "handmade" or "small-batch" or "ethical" concrete. Visuals do most of the work.

**Pitfall:** stock photos of generic workshops or factories — readers can tell. Use real photos even if they're imperfect.

---

## Block 5: The team / The maker

Bio + photo for the founder. Optional team grid for larger operations.

**Founder bio (150-250 words):**
- Photo (real, ideally working — not corporate headshot)
- Name + role
- 1-2 paragraph bio: where they came from, what they did before, why they care
- Optional: a personal aside (favorite thing they make, a hobby, a quirk that makes them feel like a person)

**Team grid (when applicable):**
- 3-12 team members with photo, first name, role, and 1-line bio
- Keep it human — quirky one-liners outperform formal titles

**Pitfall:** corporate-style team page (formal photos, titles like "Chief Brand Officer") on a brand that sells handmade goods or claims to be small. Mismatch reads as fake.

---

## Block 6: Press & recognition (optional)

Only include if the brand has been featured in real press. Never fabricate.

**Format:**
- Logos of publications/podcasts that featured the brand (with permission)
- 1-2 pull quotes from articles (with attribution)
- Awards or certifications (B Corp, organic certifications, etc.)

**Pitfall:** generic "As Seen In" logo strip where the logos are random publications that mentioned the brand in passing. Be honest about real coverage; a single substantive feature beats five trivial mentions.

---

## Block 7: What we make

Re-route the reader from the About page into the store. 3-6 entry points.

**Format:** small grid of curated collection links or featured product cards.

**Selection criteria:**
- The flagship product (whatever makes the brand famous)
- The beginner-friendly entry point (lowest-friction first purchase)
- The bestselling collection
- A seasonal or new collection (fresh thing to explore)

**Conversion job:** the reader who finished the About page is the warmest possible visitor. Give them somewhere to go.

---

## Block 8: Sign-off + call to connect

A short, human closer. Three optional elements:

- A short paragraph from the founder (50-100 words) — "thanks for reading, here's how to reach us"
- Newsletter capture (only if the brand actually emails — don't ask for emails to ignore them)
- Social handles (only the ones the brand actively posts on)
- Contact info — email or chat

**Pitfall:** treating the closer as a generic CTA block. The About page is the most personal page in the store. End it with warmth.

---

## Length targets

- Total word count: 800-2000 words
- Story section is the largest chunk (50-60% of total)
- Principles section: 250-500 words
- Behind the scenes: 200-400 words
- Bio + team: 200-500 words

About pages can be longer than this if the brand has genuine depth of story. They shouldn't be shorter — a thin About page is worse than no About page.

## Mobile

About pages on mobile are mostly read end-to-end (unlike product pages which get scanned). The mobile design should encourage scrolling:

- Single column, ample line spacing
- Hero image full-width, headline below
- Story beats with images interspersed
- No accordions on About — readers are committed when they land here

## Common pitfalls

- **No real story** — vague language that could apply to any brand in the category. Fix: extract specific moments, dates, frustrations, names.
- **Mission-statement opener** — "Our mission is to..." instantly tunes the reader out. Open with narrative.
- **Skipping the founder photo** — readers want to see the person behind the brand. Even imperfect photos win over no photo.
- **Generic stock photos of "production"** — instant trust failure. Use real photos or omit the block.
- **No call to connect** — wastes the warmest visitor in the funnel. Always end with a way forward.
- **Treating About like SEO content** — keyword-stuffed About pages read as inauthentic. About is for trust, not search.

## When the buyer has no story yet

Sometimes the buyer is launching a new brand and doesn't have an origin story or working principles. Don't fabricate. Ask 5-7 prompt questions:

1. What problem were you trying to solve when you started the brand?
2. What did you try first that didn't work?
3. Why are you the right person to make this?
4. What do you believe about [category] that the industry mostly gets wrong?
5. Who's the customer you're imagining when you make decisions?
6. What's one thing you spend on that competitors won't?
7. Where do you make / source / build, and who else is involved?

Use the answers to draft the page. If the buyer can't answer 4 of these, the brand isn't ready for an About page yet — recommend they come back after launch, when they have real customers and real stories.
