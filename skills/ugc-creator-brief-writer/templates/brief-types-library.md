# Brief Types Library

Three brief types are supported. The buyer's relationship with the creator and what the buyer will do with the content determines the type. Type drives which sections appear and how stringent the compliance language is.

---

## Type 1: Organic UGC

**Buyer relationship:** Buyer either paid the creator a flat fee (or shipped product as the only consideration) for one or more videos that the buyer will then repost on their own social channels. The creator's own social account doesn't post the content; the buyer does.

**FTC #ad requirement:** Generally no, when the buyer reposts to their own owned channel. (The buyer is posting their own marketing content.) FTC rules still apply if the creator separately posts the same content on their own account — but that's the creator's compliance burden if they post it organically.

**Use case:** Most cost-effective UGC at scale. Cheaper rates than paid usage. The video lives on the buyer's account.

### Sections (in order)

1. **Project title** — "Organic UGC: [Product] for [Brand] — [Platform(s)]"
2. **Hi, [creator name]** — warm 2-sentence intro
3. **What we're making** — what the content is, where it'll live (only on the brand's social, not on the creator's)
4. **About the product** — 4-6 sentences in brand voice
5. **Who this is for** — audience description (the people watching the final video)
6. **The ask** — 1-2 sentences: what the buyer wants from this content
7. **10 hooks across 5 archetypes** — see `hook-archetypes.md`
8. **B-roll shot list** — 5-10 specific shots — see `bsroll-shotlist-patterns.md`
9. **Talking points** — 3-5 things to mention organically (NOT a script)
10. **Tone of voice** — register, energy, what to avoid
11. **Do's and don'ts** — 5-7 each
12. **Deliverables** — number of videos, per-platform specs, durations, aspect ratios
13. **Timeline** — shoot deadline, deliver-by deadline
14. **How to deliver** — Frame.io, Dropbox, WeTransfer
15. **What we'll do with it** — confirms organic-only use, NOT ads

### Tone notes for the brief itself

- Warm and direct
- Doesn't read corporate (creators have agency contracts; they don't need another)
- Acknowledges the creator's craft ("we're hiring you for your eye, not just your equipment")
- Avoids over-specification (kills creator ownership and reduces quality)

### Common pitfall

Buyers often over-write organic UGC briefs ("shoot this exact frame, say these exact words"). That kills the creator's ownership and produces worse content than letting them riff. The brief should set guardrails, not write the video.

## Type 2: Paid UGC (Spark Ads / Whitelisted Ads)

**Buyer relationship:** Buyer wants to run the video as a paid ad — either through their own ad account (Spark Ads, where Meta/TikTok let the buyer boost an organic creator post) or through the creator's own handle (whitelisted / allowlisted, where the buyer's ad runs as the creator's account).

**FTC #ad requirement:** Yes, always. The video must include #ad, #sponsored, or "Paid partnership with [brand]" disclosure as required by the FTC. Specific platform requirements vary slightly (Meta and TikTok have their own disclosure UIs).

**Usage rights:** Must be explicit. The buyer is licensing rights to run the footage as paid media for a specific duration. Without explicit rights, the buyer can't legally run the ads.

**Use case:** Highest-converting UGC because the buyer can amplify it with paid spend. Most expensive (creators charge usage on top of production fee). Most compliance-heavy.

### Sections (in order)

All the Organic sections, PLUS:

13b. **FTC disclosure language** — exact wording the creator must include in the video and the caption (see `ftc-and-rights.md`)
13c. **Brand-safety guardrails** — things the creator must NEVER say or imply that could trigger ad rejection (see "Compliance" subsection below)
13d. **Usage rights paragraph** — what the buyer is licensed to do, where, and for how long (3 months, 6 months, perpetual). Includes whether the buyer can use cuts, remixes, or just the original video.
13e. **Whitelisting handshake** (when whitelisted) — instructions for the creator to enable the buyer's ad account access on Meta/TikTok. Step-by-step.
13f. **Approval flow** — the creator must send the buyer the raw video for sign-off before the ad goes live.

### Tone notes for paid brief

- Slightly more formal than organic (legal context matters)
- Still warm; still creator-respecting
- Compliance language stated factually, not threateningly
- Frame guardrails as protecting the campaign's effectiveness ("if your video says X, Meta will reject the ad and we both lose")

### Compliance — common ad-rejection triggers

When the creator's content gets used as paid media, the platforms (Meta, TikTok) auto-review it. These triggers cause rejections:

- **Personal attributes**: "you're overweight," "as a depressed person," "for [skin color/condition] people" — even when framed positively. Meta's policy bans implying personal attributes of the viewer.
- **Before/after with body / face shots**: especially for beauty, fitness, weight-loss categories. Allowed only with strict guardrails.
- **Health / medical claims**: even mild ones ("clears acne," "stops migraines") get flagged.
- **Misleading "results"**: typical disclaimer required.
- **Profanity** (even bleeped): TikTok is stricter than Meta here.
- **References to other platforms**: "follow me on Instagram" in a TikTok ad gets flagged.
- **Crisis topics referenced casually**: war, disasters, addiction — even sympathetically — can trigger sensitive-content flags on auto-review.

The brief should call these out as "things to avoid even when they feel benign" — creators not trained in paid-social compliance often surface these accidentally.

### Common pitfall

Buyers send a brief that reads exactly like an organic brief, then try to run the video as an ad. The creator's content includes prohibited claims, the ad gets rejected, and the buyer either loses the spend or has to commission a re-shoot. Always specify upfront whether the content will be paid.

## Type 3: Affiliate / Commission Content

**Buyer relationship:** Creator earns commission on sales they drive via a tracked link or discount code. Usually no flat fee, or a small flat fee + commission. Content lives on the creator's account.

**FTC #ad requirement:** Yes — affiliate relationships must be disclosed. "Affiliate" or "#ad" both count for FTC purposes; some buyer brands prefer "affiliate" since it more accurately describes the relationship.

**On-screen requirements:** The tracked link or discount code must be visible on screen (typical platform pattern: pinned comment, swipe-up sticker, or code on-screen text overlay).

**Use case:** Lowest upfront cost. Aligns creator incentive with sales. Best when the buyer has a recognizable brand or a discount that's actually attractive.

### Sections (in order)

All the Organic sections, PLUS:

13b. **Affiliate disclosure language** — "#affiliate," "[brand] affiliate," or platform-specific affiliate disclosure
13c. **Tracked link** — the exact URL, and where it goes (pinned comment is most common)
13d. **Discount code** — the exact code, when to mention it, where to display it on-screen
13e. **Commission structure** — % of sale, how often payout, how the creator sees the dashboard
13f. **On-screen display requirements** — when in the video the link/code appears, for how long, in what position (typically: code shown on-screen for the final 5 seconds; link in pinned comment)

### Tone notes for affiliate brief

- More partnership-tone than transactional ("we win when you win")
- Detail on the dashboard / payout structure is appreciated by creators (most affiliate programs are murky)
- Specify whether the creator can re-use the discount code in other videos beyond this brief

### Common pitfall

Buyers offer 5% commission on a $30 product and wonder why creators don't drive sales. The brief should NOT try to recruit — that's outside scope. But if the buyer's commission structure is uncompetitive (under 15% for most lifestyle DTC), the brief might note "the rate determines who'll take this."

## What's the same across all three types

Regardless of type, every brief:

- Reads like a real human wrote it (not a template)
- Respects the creator's craft and ownership
- Sets clear guardrails without writing the video
- Includes hooks the creator can mix-and-match
- Lists b-roll specifically enough that the creator knows what to shoot
- Has a clear timeline and delivery process
- Mentions the brand voice but doesn't try to make the creator sound like the brand (creators sound like themselves; that's the point of UGC)

## Type detection from user language

When the user requests a brief, infer the type:

| Language signal | Type |
|---|---|
| "for my own social," "I want to repost it," "for our Instagram" | Organic |
| "Spark Ads," "I'll boost it," "I'll run it as an ad," "for ads," "whitelisted," "allowlisted," "ads through their handle" | Paid |
| "affiliate," "commission," "tracked link," "discount code," "earn a percentage" | Affiliate |
| "I'm seeding product" | Treat as Organic (seeding briefs are lightweight organic briefs — the buyer has no enforceable expectations on the creator) |
| Unclear | Ask: "Is this for organic (you'll repost), paid (you'll run as an ad), or affiliate (creator earns commission)?" |

When multiple types are mentioned ("I want to repost AND maybe run as Spark"), default to paid — paid requirements are the strictest, and content that meets paid requirements is fine for organic too.
